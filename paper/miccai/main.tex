\title{Whoever Picks the Tiles Decides: Selection Attacks on Efficient Whole-Slide Classifiers}
\titlerunning{Selection Attacks on Efficient Whole-Slide Classifiers}
\author{Anonymized Authors}\authorrunning{Anonymized Author et al.}\institute{Anonymized Affiliations\\\email{email@anonymized.com}}

\begin{abstract}
Efficient whole-slide image (WSI) classifiers use a cheap model to pick a few tiles and encode only those with a pathology foundation model, reaching near full-slide accuracy at a fraction of the cost. We show that this tile selector is a single point of failure. An attacker who controls only the selector, and shows nothing but real, unmodified tiles from the slide, decides the diagnosis: on CAMELYON17, TCGA-RCC and TCGA-NSCLC, a compromised selector drives an ADMIL-style pipeline to the attacker's class on nearly every slide ($\kappa=-.24$; balanced accuracy .02 and .00). A larger tile budget does not help, while an honest but weak selector costs at most .01, so the failure is specific to adversarial selection. Debate-style presenters, with one advocate per diagnosis, do not solve the problem, because the true class's advocate is itself a single point of failure. We show that plain redundancy does: presenting the selector's top tiles in several interchangeable slots restores worst-case performance to .65--.74 on all three cohorts, with clean accuracy equal to the single selector and only $K_c$ more tiles encoded per slide.
\keywords{Multiple-instance learning \and Adversarial robustness \and Efficient WSI inference}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Consider a lymph-node staging model deployed in a digital pathology workflow. To keep inference fast, the slide is first scanned at low magnification by a lightweight triage model, which hands the 10--20 most relevant tiles to a foundation-model classifier. The triage model is often a separate component: supplied by a different vendor, retrained on a different schedule, or updated independently of the classifier it feeds. Every tile it hands over is a real region of the patient's slide, so nothing in the output looks wrong to a pathologist reviewing the evidence. Yet whoever controls that hand-off controls what the classifier sees. \inprog{scene to refine with clinical input}

Such pipelines are increasingly common. Encoding every tile of a gigapixel WSI with a pathology foundation model~\cite{uni,conch} dominates the cost of slide-level inference, so efficient methods select first and aggregate second~\cite{thandiackal2022zoommil,zhao2023rlogist,admil2026,eagle2026}. In our experiments, a selector that passes 8--16 of 2,000--4,300 tiles (0.2--0.8\%) to a multiple-instance learning (MIL) model stays within .014--.026 of full-slide MIL.

We ask how robust these pipelines are when the selector cannot be trusted. Our attacker is deliberately restricted: it never perturbs a pixel or a feature and shows no more tiles than the honest selector. It only chooses \emph{which} real tiles of the slide are presented. Existing attacks and defences for MIL operate on pixels or features~\cite{foote2021now,mil_adv2022} and do not cover this threat, because every presented tile is genuine.

\textbf{Contributions.} (1) We formalise tile-selection attacks on efficient WSI classifiers, with a per-tile and a judge-aware adaptive attacker. (2) We show that the single-selector pipeline collapses under this attack on three cohorts and two tasks (metastasis staging, cancer subtyping), at every tile budget we test, although it is insensitive to honest selector quality. (3) We show that debate-style per-class advocates fail when the true class's advocate is compromised, and characterise when a presenter can survive one compromise (Prop.~\ref{prop:witness}). (4) We show that simple redundant presentation restores robustness across all cohorts at negligible cost, and compare joint and isolated aggregation over 3 seeds $\times$ 5 folds.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Efficient WSI inference.} MIL on foundation-model features~\cite{ilse2018abmil,lu2021data,shao2021transmil,li2021dual} reads every tile. ZoomMIL~\cite{thandiackal2022zoommil}, RLogist~\cite{zhao2023rlogist}, ADMIL~\cite{admil2026} and EAGLE~\cite{eagle2026} select a few tiles with a cheap model first. Recent work studies how selection shifts the evidence MIL sees~\cite{bagshift2026,foci2026} and regularises attention to be faithful~\cite{gcemil2026,carmil2026}; the selector is assumed to be trustworthy.
\textbf{Adversarial robustness in pathology.} Attacks on MIL perturb tile pixels or features~\cite{foote2021now,mil_adv2022}. Our attacker uses only authentic tiles, which pixel-level defences cannot flag.
\textbf{Debate and redundancy.} Debate~\cite{irving2018debate,khan2024debate}, prover--verifier games~\cite{anil2021pvg} and Merlin--Arthur classifiers~\cite{waldchen2024merlin} let untrusted provers present evidence to a verifier; per-class advocates follow this idea. Isolate-then-aggregate defences judge each retrieved passage or image region separately~\cite{xiang2024robustrag,xiang2022patchcleanser}; we evaluate the WSI analogue alongside joint aggregation.

\section{Method}
\textbf{Setting.} A slide $x$ with label $y$ has tiles $\{t_1,\dots,t_N\}$. A \emph{presenter} fills slots $s=1..m$; slot $s$ shows a tile set $S_s$ with $|S_s|\le B_s$, each tile tagged with a one-hot slot flag. The judge $J$, a gated-attention MIL model~\cite{ilse2018abmil} over the foundation-model features of the shown tiles, outputs class probabilities $p_J(\cdot\mid S_1,\dots,S_m)$.

\textbf{Threat model.} The attacker compromises \emph{one} slot of its choice and replaces $S_s$ with any $B_s$ tiles of the same slide; it cannot change the other slots, the flags or the features. We give it the true label and white-box access to $J$, the strongest setting. \emph{Per-tile attack}: for each wrong class $c$, show the $B_s$ tiles with the highest single-tile $\log p_J(c)$ under slot $s$'s flag. \emph{Adaptive attack}: build $S_s$ greedily from the top-128 per-tile candidates, adding at each step the tile that minimises $p_J(y)$ given the honest slots. We report the worst case per slide over slots, target classes and both attacks.

\textbf{Presenters.} \emph{Single selector} (ADMIL-style~\cite{admil2026}): one slot with the top-$K$ tiles by the attention of a cheap scorer, an MLP on low-magnification EfficientNet-B0~\cite{tan2019efficientnet} features distilled from a full-slide CONCH~\cite{conch} ABMIL; $K=C K_c$ with $K_c=4$. \emph{Advocates}: one slot per class $c$ with the $K_c$ tiles of largest cheap-scorer margin for $c$ (stage-specific spatial rules on CAMELYON17; see supplement). \emph{Advocates + selector slot}: the advocates plus one unlabelled slot with the selector's top $K_c$ or $K$ tiles. \emph{Redundancy}: $m=C{+}1$ interchangeable slots that receive the selector's top $mK_c$ tiles round-robin, aggregated either \emph{jointly} by one judge over all slots or \emph{isolated}, by judging each slot alone and averaging log-probabilities~\cite{xiang2024robustrag}.

\textbf{Judge training.} Multi-slot judges are trained on honest presentations and on presentations with one slot replaced by the per-tile attacker, for three rounds, over every (slot, target class) pair available to the attacker. The single selector's judge is the full-slide model.

\textbf{When can a presenter survive one compromise?} Call a slot a \emph{witness} for $(x,y)$ if it is honest and its tiles are chosen without regard to any wrong class: the advocate of $y$, the selector slot, or a redundant slot.
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:witness}
(i) The single selector is robust on $(x,y)$ exactly when no set of at most $K$ tiles of the slide leads $J$ to a wrong class. (ii) For advocates, compromising the advocate of $y$ removes every witness; all remaining slots are chosen to support wrong classes. (iii) Advocates + selector slot and redundancy with $m\ge2$ slots retain at least one witness under any single compromise. With isolated aggregation, the presenter is robust on $(x,y)$ exactly when, for every slot $s$ and wrong class $c$, $\sum_{s'\neq s}\big[\log p_J(y\mid S_{s'})-\log p_J(c\mid S_{s'})\big] > \max_{|S|\le B_s}\big[\log p_J(c\mid S)-\log p_J(y\mid S)\big]$.
\end{proposition}
Part (i) explains the collapse: in heterogeneous tissue, some $K$ tiles almost always resemble another class. Part (ii) explains why advocates fail exactly where it matters. Part (iii) is the structural property that redundancy provides; whether the judge follows the remaining witness is what our worst-case evaluation measures.

\section{Experiments}
\textbf{Data.} CAMELYON17~\cite{camelyon17} (500 lymph-node slides, 4 pN stages, leave-one-hospital-out, quadratic-weighted $\kappa$), TCGA-RCC (882 slides, 3 subtypes) and TCGA-NSCLC (1,041 slides, 2 subtypes)~\cite{tcga} (5-fold patient-level CV, balanced accuracy). Tiles are encoded at 20$\times$ with CONCH and at low magnification with EfficientNet-B0. We report the mean over 5 folds, then mean $\pm$ sd over 3 seeds; paired 95\% bootstrap CIs are over the 15 fold-seed runs.

\textbf{The single selector collapses} (Tab.~\ref{tab:main}). Compromised, it predicts the attacker's class on almost every slide: $\kappa=-.24$ on CAMELYON17 and .02/.00 balanced accuracy on RCC/NSCLC, consistently across folds and seeds. A larger budget does not help ($-$.21/.04/.01 at $2K$ tiles). At the same time the pipeline is insensitive to honest selector quality: a cheap scorer distilled from only 10\% of the training slides costs $\le$.01. The vulnerability is therefore specific to adversarial selection. \inprog{Tab.~2: the same attack against mean-pooling, max-pooling, TransMIL-style and DSMIL-style judges (running)}

\textbf{Advocates fail at the true advocate.} As predicted by Prop.~\ref{prop:witness}(ii), compromising the advocate of $y$ leaves the judge with evidence for wrong classes only, and the worst case falls to .59/.43/.15. Training an independent scorer for each advocate, so that an attacker would have to compromise them separately, does not change this (.68/.68/.51 even under the weaker per-tile attack).

\textbf{Redundancy restores robustness.} Joint redundancy keeps .654/.739/.725 under the strongest attack, up from $-$.241/.022/.003 for the single selector, while matching its clean accuracy (.869/.915/.914 vs.\ .873/.907/.909). Adding a selector slot to the advocates reaches a similar worst case (.705/.713/.665) with the highest clean accuracy on CAMELYON17 and RCC; joint redundancy is significantly more robust on NSCLC (+.060, 95\% CI [.042, .080]). Isolated aggregation gives the best worst case on CAMELYON17 (.748), but joint aggregation is more reliable across cohorts. We therefore recommend joint redundancy as the default: it needs no class-specific selection rules and no change to the selector.

\textbf{Cost.} Redundancy encodes $(C{+}1)K_c$ instead of $CK_c$ tiles (20/16/12 vs.\ 16/12/8, out of thousands). Encoding the selected tiles takes $<$0.3\,s of a 5.5--7.9\,s pipeline that is dominated by reading the slide at low magnification, so the overhead is negligible.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
Efficient WSI classifiers concentrate trust in the tile selector, a component that is accurate when honest but decides the diagnosis when compromised. Because the attack uses only authentic tiles, it is invisible to pixel-level defences and to a reviewer who checks the presented evidence tile by tile. Debate-style advocates, a natural fix, inherit the same weakness at the true class's advocate. Redundant presentation removes this single point of failure at negligible cost and is a drop-in change to existing pipelines. Extending the analysis to several colluding components and to attackers without label or model access are natural next steps.
